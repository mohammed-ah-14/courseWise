How It Works

In short: we look at how a course, its instructor and its subject have graded in the past, and a model turns that history into an estimate for the next term.

The pipeline

From raw grades to a prediction

  1. 1Raw gradesOne row per section: counts of A+ to F and withdrawals.
  2. 2Clean & mergeSections of the same course, instructor and term become one offering.
  3. 3History featuresOnly earlier terms are used, so the model never sees the future.
  4. 4Train & compareNine model types, tuned with time-based cross-validation.
  5. 5Test onceThe winner is scored on terms it never saw.
Splitting by time

Trained on the past, tested on the future

A random split would let the model peek at later terms. Splitting by year is how it would be used for real.

Data

The dataset

Public grade distributions of the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign, –, compiled by Prof. Wade Fagen-Ulmschneider, joined with the course catalogue for credit hours and Gen-Ed categories.

Limits

What to keep in mind

  • Averages, not individuals. A predicted 3.0 means the class averages 3.0. Many students will be above or below.
  • New courses and new instructors have little history, so their predictions lean on the subject average and are less certain.
  • Different university. The data is from Illinois. Grading cultures differ, so numbers would shift for Sharjah, though the method stays the same.
  • Fairness. Instructor names are used because they improve accuracy. The site shows them only to describe past courses, not to rank people.
